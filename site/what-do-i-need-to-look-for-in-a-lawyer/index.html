What Do I Need to Look For in a Lawyer?

By  Akiva Shapiro, Esq. Published 2018 · Last reviewed August 2026

Look for a lawyer with real experience in your specific type of problem and in the courts where it will play out, clear written fee terms, and communication you can live with for the life of the case. Verify that the attorney is admitted and in good standing in New York, ask who will handle the day-to-day work, and walk away from anyone who guarantees a result, because no honest lawyer can.

Experience That Matches Your Problem

Law is specialized, and the match matters more than the brand. A brilliant real estate closer may be lost in a contested estate case; a corporate deal lawyer may never have taken a deposition. Ask directly: have you handled matters like mine, how did they tend to resolve, and what do you see as the hard parts of my situation?

Local knowledge counts too. A lawyer who regularly appears in the courts where your case will live, whether that is Nassau County Supreme Court, Surrogate's Court, or the District Courts on Long Island, knows the judges' expectations, the local rules, and the practical rhythms that never appear in statutes. For disputes that touch several fields, such as an estate fight with business assets, look for counsel comfortable across those lines. And confirm the basics: New York attorney registration is public, so you can verify admission and standing yourself.

Credentials beyond the courtroom can matter as well. A lawyer with business training or hands-on operational experience may read a commercial dispute differently, and an attorney who writes or teaches in a field tends to know it deeply. Weigh those signals alongside the case experience, though none replaces the fundamental question: has this lawyer handled problems like yours?

Fees, Engagement Letters, and Communication

Money conversations belong at the start. Understand the structure, hourly against a retainer, flat fee, or contingency, and what happens when the retainer runs low. New York court rules generally require attorneys to provide a written engagement letter or retainer agreement describing the fees and the scope of the work, so expect one and read it before signing. Ask what is billed, how often you will see invoices, and which expenses, like filing fees, transcripts, and experts, are yours on top of fees.

Then probe communication. Who actually works the file, the lawyer you met or someone you have not? How quickly are calls and emails returned? Will you be consulted before significant moves and expenses? A lawsuit is a long relationship, and unreturned phone calls are among the most common grievances clients report about lawyers. Choose someone whose communication habits you can verify early, because they rarely improve later.

Red Flags and Green Flags

Watch for warning signs during the first consultation:

  • Guaranteed outcomes or promises about what a judge will do. Results depend on facts, law, and people no one controls.
  • Pressure to sign immediately, or vagueness about fees and scope.
  • Trash-talking every other lawyer and promising maximum aggression. Bluster is not strategy.
  • No questions about your documents or goals. A lawyer who does not probe cannot evaluate.

The green flags are quieter. Good counsel asks hard questions, tells you things you did not want to hear, explains options including the option of not litigating, gives a candid range of outcomes and costs, and puts everything in writing. Trust builds from candor, and candor in the first meeting predicts candor when it matters most, mid-case, when real decisions have to be made.

Key Takeaways

  • Match the lawyer's actual experience to your specific problem and the local courts.
  • Expect a written engagement letter describing fees and scope; New York rules generally require one.
  • Know who will do the work and how quickly communications are answered.
  • A guaranteed result is a red flag, not a selling point.
  • Candid risk assessment and clear writing are the best predictors of a good relationship.
