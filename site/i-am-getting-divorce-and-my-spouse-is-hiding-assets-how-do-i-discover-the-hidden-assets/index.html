I Am Getting Divorced and My Spouse Is Hiding Assets. How Do I Discover the Hidden Assets?

By  Akiva Shapiro, Esq. Published 2019 · Last reviewed August 2026

New York divorce law gives you powerful tools to uncover hidden assets. Both spouses must exchange sworn financial disclosure, and your attorney can demand documents, question your spouse under oath, and subpoena banks, employers, and business records directly. Courts punish concealment: a judge can sanction a spouse who hides assets, draw negative inferences against them, and award you a larger share of whatever was hidden.

Financial Disclosure Is Mandatory in a New York Divorce

In a contested New York divorce, each spouse must complete a statement of net worth — a sworn, detailed accounting of income, expenses, assets, and debts, signed under penalty of perjury and typically backed by tax returns and pay records. This is not optional, and it is the foundation the rest of the case is built on.

That sworn statement is also a trap for a dishonest spouse. Every omission and every undervalued asset becomes documented evidence. When the hidden account or the understated business income later surfaces, the false disclosure itself undermines your spouse's credibility on every other issue in the case, from property division to custody.

New York also protects the marital pot while the case is pending. Once a divorce begins, automatic orders restrain both spouses from transferring, hiding, or borrowing against marital assets outside the ordinary course of living. A spouse who moves money anyway is violating a court order, with consequences to match. Tell your attorney immediately about any transfer you suspect happened just before filing — pre-divorce transfers can be traced and clawed back into the marital estate.

Discovery Tools That Uncover Hidden Money

Beyond the required disclosure, New York's discovery process lets your attorney dig:

  • Document demands for bank statements, brokerage records, loan applications, credit card statements, and business books going back years.
  • Subpoenas served directly on banks, employers, business partners, and financial institutions — so you are not relying on your spouse's honesty.
  • Depositions, where your spouse must answer questions under oath, on the record.
  • Forensic accountants, who trace transfers, compare reported income against lifestyle, and analyze cash businesses for skimmed or deferred revenue.

Loan applications are often gold: people tend to overstate assets to lenders and understate them in divorce, and the contradiction is hard to explain away.

Timing helps too. The earlier discovery starts, the less time there is to bury transactions, and the easier it is for a forensic accountant to follow money that has not yet been layered through multiple accounts.

Red Flags — and What Happens to a Spouse Who Hides Assets

Common warning signs include sudden repayment of vague loans to relatives or friends, a cash business whose reported income drops as the divorce approaches, new accounts your spouse never mentions, transfers to cryptocurrency, deliberately overpaid taxes waiting to come back as a refund, and bonuses or commissions conveniently deferred until after the divorce.

New York judges have real remedies for concealment. A court can sanction the hiding spouse, order them to pay your attorney's fees, draw an adverse inference that values the hidden asset against them, and award you a greater share of the marital estate. Even after the divorce is final, a settlement or judgment obtained through financial fraud can be reopened. Hiding assets is not clever strategy — it is a liability that compounds.

One caution as you gather proof: stick to lawful self-help. Copy the statements, tax returns, and account records you already have access to as a spouse, and preserve texts and emails — but do not guess passwords, log into your spouse's private accounts, or install tracking software. Illegally obtained evidence can poison your case. Bring what you have to your attorney and let the discovery process do the heavy lifting.

Key Takeaways

  • Both spouses in a New York divorce must file a sworn statement of net worth.
  • Subpoenas, depositions, and document demands reach records your spouse will not volunteer.
  • Forensic accountants trace transfers and compare reported income against actual lifestyle.
  • Loan applications often contradict divorce disclosures and expose hidden wealth.
  • Courts can sanction concealment and award the honest spouse a larger share.
