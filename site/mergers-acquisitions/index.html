The Deal Roadmap: LOI to Closing

Most business sales follow the same arc:

  • Letter of intent. A short, mostly non-binding outline of price and key terms. It carries more weight than its label suggests, because it anchors everything negotiated afterward.
  • Due diligence. The buyer's structured examination of the business: financials, contracts, leases, employees, litigation, licenses, and liabilities.
  • The purchase agreement. The binding contract that allocates every risk the diligence uncovered.
  • Consents and approvals. Third parties who must sign off, most often the landlord consenting to a lease assignment; the location is often much of what is being bought.
  • Closing. Funds move, documents are exchanged, and ownership transfers, along with the transition arrangements that make the handoff work.

A rushed LOI narrows the diligence, thin diligence weakens the agreement, and a weak agreement haunts the closing.

Asset Sale or Stock Sale, and Why It Matters

Every deal starts with a structural fork. In a stock or equity sale, the buyer purchases ownership of the company itself and takes it whole, liabilities included. In an asset sale, the buyer purchases selected assets, the equipment, name, customer lists, and goodwill, and leaves the corporate shell and most of its liabilities behind with the seller.

Small-business deals in New York are usually asset sales, for a reason: buyers do not want to inherit unknown debts, lawsuits, or tax problems, and an asset deal lets them start clean. Sellers often prefer equity sales for tax and simplicity, so structure becomes a negotiated term with real dollars attached.

New York adds a compliance wrinkle: notice of the bulk sale to the state tax department, which protects a buyer from responsibility for the seller's unpaid sales taxes. Skipping it is an expensive mistake.

Inside the Purchase Agreement

The purchase agreement is where diligence findings become enforceable protection. The provisions that matter most:

  • Representations and warranties. The seller's formal statements about the business: financials accurate, taxes paid, no hidden lawsuits. In plain terms, the promises the price is based on.
  • Indemnification. Who pays, and up to how much, if a promise proves false after closing.
  • Escrows and holdbacks. A slice of the price parked with a neutral party or withheld for a period, giving the buyer a source of recovery without suing.
  • Non-competes and non-solicits. The seller's agreement not to open across the street or take back customers and staff. New York courts generally enforce reasonable restrictions given as part of a business sale.
  • Transition terms. Training, introductions, and the seller's short-term involvement after closing.

These provisions are where a good deal is protected and a bad one is survived.

Seller Financing: When the Seller Is the Bank

Many Long Island deals close with the seller financing part of the price: a portion paid at closing, the rest over time under a promissory note.

For sellers, the note is only as good as its protections: a personal guaranty from the buyer, a security interest in the business assets, perfected by a Uniform Commercial Code lien filing, meaningful default terms, and sometimes the right to step back in if payments stop. Without them, an unpaid seller is an unsecured creditor of a business someone else now runs.

For buyers, seller financing keeps the seller invested in your success, but the note's covenants and default triggers deserve the same scrutiny as a bank loan. The note is not paperwork; it is the deal's second act.

An Operator's Read on the Numbers

Legal diligence answers whether the business is what the papers say. An operator's diligence asks whether the business is what the price says.

That means reading the financials critically: how dependent revenue is on a few customers, whether the owner's claimed add-backs to earnings hold up, what the lease will do to margins, what payroll looks like once family members' roles are normalized, and whether the equipment on the books matches the floor.

Key Takeaways

  • Most Long Island small-business deals are asset sales; buyers take the assets and leave the liabilities.
  • The LOI anchors the deal; get counsel involved before it is signed, not after.
  • Reps, warranties, indemnification, and escrow are what make the seller's promises collectible.
  • New York's bulk sale notice protects buyers from the seller's unpaid sales taxes.
  • Seller financing without a guaranty and a perfected lien is a promise, not a protection.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an asset sale and a stock sale?

In a stock or equity sale, the buyer purchases the company itself, taking its assets and its liabilities. In an asset sale, the buyer purchases selected assets and leaves the legal entity, and most of its liabilities, with the seller. Buyers usually prefer asset deals for the clean start; sellers often prefer equity deals. Most small-business sales in New York are structured as asset sales.

Why do I need due diligence if I trust the seller?

Because the seller does not know everything either. Diligence regularly surfaces problems sellers were unaware of: a lease clause blocking assignment, a tax exposure, an expired license, a customer contract that terminates on a change of ownership. Trust is not the issue. Verification converts the seller's honest beliefs into documented facts, and determines the protections you need in the purchase agreement.

What is an escrow or holdback in a business sale?

It is part of the purchase price that does not reach the seller at closing. The funds sit with a neutral escrow agent, or are simply withheld by the buyer, for an agreed period after the sale. If a seller's representation proves false or an indemnification claim arises, the buyer recovers from that fund instead of chasing the seller in court.

Is a non-compete enforceable when selling a business in New York?

Generally, yes, when it is reasonable. New York courts treat non-competes signed as part of a business sale far more favorably than those imposed on employees, because the buyer paid for the goodwill the restriction protects. The limits must still be sensible in duration, geography, and scope. A carefully drafted restriction protects the buyer's investment; an overreaching one invites a challenge.

Should I accept seller financing when I sell my business?

It can be a sound choice, and in many small-business sales it is the only way a deal gets done, but only with real protections: a personal guaranty from the buyer, a perfected security interest in the business assets, and clear default remedies. Structured well, the note can also improve your overall price. Structured poorly, you have handed over your business for a promise.
