How Do I Find an Estate Planning Attorney in New York?

By  Akiva Shapiro, Esq. Published 2018 · Last reviewed August 2026

The best way to find an estate planning attorney in New York is through referrals from people who see this work up close — your accountant, your financial advisor, or friends and family who recently signed their own plans — followed by a consultation before you commit. Look for a lawyer who concentrates on wills, trusts, and estates, knows Surrogate's Court practice, and explains your options in plain English.

Watch Akiva answer this question

Where to Look for Estate Planning Help

Start with professionals who watch estate plans succeed or fail. Accountants and financial advisors see whose documents actually work when a client dies or becomes incapacitated, and they tend to refer carefully because their own reputation rides on it.

Other reliable sources include friends or family who recently served as an executor — they experienced firsthand how well a plan was built — and bar association lawyer referral services, including the New York State Bar Association and the county bar associations serving Nassau and Suffolk on Long Island. An attorney's own articles, books, and seminars can also signal genuine depth in the field rather than occasional dabbling.

Be cautious with the loudest marketing. High-pressure trust seminars, one-size-fits-all document packages, and non-lawyer companies selling fill-in-the-blank forms all exist because estate planning looks deceptively simple. The documents are only as good as the judgment behind them, and New York's execution formalities leave little room for error. A referral is a starting point, not a verdict — verify that any attorney you consider is admitted in New York and in good standing.

What to Evaluate Before You Hire

Estate planning is a poor fit for a generalist. New York has its own estate tax separate from the federal system, its own Medicaid rules for long-term care planning, and strict signing formalities that can invalidate documents executed incorrectly. You want someone who works in this area constantly, not occasionally.

Experience in Surrogate's Court — the New York court that handles probate and estate administration — matters more than most people realize. A lawyer who regularly probates wills and administers estates knows exactly which drafting choices cause problems later and builds plans that hold up. Local familiarity helps too: an attorney who practices where you live understands the courts and county-level procedures your family will actually deal with.

Firm size matters far less than attention. What you want is a lawyer who will genuinely think about your family's situation — solo practitioners and small firms often provide exactly that, with direct access to the attorney doing the work rather than a team you never meet.

Questions to Ask at the First Consultation

A short list of direct questions will tell you most of what you need to know:

  • Is estate planning the core of your practice, or one service among many?
  • Who will draft my documents, and who will answer my questions along the way?
  • How do you charge, and exactly what does the engagement include?
  • Will you help me retitle assets and fund my trust, or is that left to me?
  • How do reviews and updates work after I sign?

Then weigh the intangibles. A good estate planning attorney asks about your family, your assets, and your worries before recommending a single document — and translates every legal term as they go. If you leave the meeting more confused than you arrived, keep looking.

Finally, think of this as choosing a long-term advisor, not buying a document. Your plan will need reviews and updates over the years, and your family may lean on this same attorney during a probate or a health crisis. Choose someone you would be comfortable calling on a hard day.

Key Takeaways

  • Referrals from accountants, financial advisors, and recent executors are the strongest leads.
  • Look for a practice concentrated in wills, trusts, and estates — not occasional dabbling.
  • Surrogate's Court experience shows an attorney knows how documents perform after death.
  • New York's separate estate tax, Medicaid rules, and signing formalities demand state-specific knowledge.
  • Ask who drafts, what is included, and whether trust funding help is part of the engagement.
