A Personal Attorney, Like a Personal Physician

Most legal problems do not arrive neatly labeled. A dispute with a contractor touches your savings. A parent's health crisis touches the family home. A marriage touches everything. When each issue goes to a different lawyer, nobody sees the whole patient — and advice that fixes one problem can quietly create another.

A personal attorney model solves that. One counselor learns your full picture once, then brings that context to every question that follows. Small questions get answered before they become expensive ones, and bigger matters start with a running head start instead of a cold file.

Marriage and Family

The legal side of love deserves the same care as the emotional side. Before the wedding — or after it — prenuptial and postnuptial agreements let couples settle financial questions themselves, protect a business or inheritance, and provide for children from a prior marriage.

And when a marriage ends, steady counsel matters more than ever. The firm's divorce and family law practice guides you through property division, support, and parenting arrangements with an eye on the years ahead, not just the fight in front of you.

Your Home and Property

For most families, real estate is the biggest asset on the books — and a steady source of legal questions. If you own rental property or lease space for a business, the firm's landlord-tenant practice represents small landlords and commercial tenants through leases, disputes, and court proceedings.

If you own a home in Nassau County, there is a yearly opportunity most owners waste: challenging an inflated assessment through a property tax grievance, with no risk of the assessment going up for filing. Even your car has its own remedy — New York's Lemon Law protects buyers stuck with a defective vehicle.

Planning Ahead: Assets, Care, and the People You Protect

The best legal outcomes are usually arranged years in advance. Through trusts and asset protection planning, you can shield what you have built from lawsuits, creditors, and the cost of long-term care.

Long-term care itself is one of the largest financial threats a family faces. Medicaid trusts and applications position families to qualify for care benefits without losing everything first — and when the system says no unfairly, Medicaid fair hearings exist to challenge the denial.

When a loved one can no longer manage their own affairs and no plan is in place, guardianship proceedings allow a court to appoint a trusted decision-maker to step in.

When Disputes Happen

Sometimes the problem cannot be planned away — a broken contract, an unpaid debt, a partner or neighbor who refuses to do the right thing. The firm's civil litigation practice represents individuals and small businesses in New York courts, pressing claims and defending against them.

Because your attorney already knows your finances and your goals, litigation decisions get made the way they should be: as life and business decisions. Some fights are worth settling early; some are worth winning. Knowing which is which is the real value of counsel.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does having a personal attorney actually mean?

It means one lawyer maintains an ongoing picture of your family, finances, and goals — the way a primary care physician maintains your medical history — and counsels you as legal events arise. Instead of re-explaining your situation to a new firm every time, you call someone who already knows the background and can see how one decision affects everything else.

Do I need a different lawyer for every kind of legal problem?

Often, no. Many of the legal events in an ordinary life — marriage agreements, property matters, planning, elder care, disputes — sit within one coordinated practice. Some specialized matters do call for specialized counsel, and an honest attorney tells you when that is the case. But continuity, wherever it is possible, produces better and less expensive advice.

When should I first talk to an attorney — before or after a problem?

Before, whenever you can. Prenups are signed before weddings, trusts are built before creditors appear, and Medicaid planning works best years ahead of a nursing home need. Legal work done in advance is planning; legal work done afterward is damage control, and it costs more. If a major life event is coming, that is the moment to get advice.

What life events should prompt a legal check-in?

Getting engaged or married, buying property or renting it out, starting a business, having children, a family member's health decline, receiving an inheritance, and any brewing dispute. Each of these changes your legal exposure or your opportunities. A short conversation at the right moment often prevents the expensive version of the same conversation later.

How does the Life practice connect to estate planning?

Seamlessly — that is the point. The Life practice handles legal events while you are living; the firm's estate and legacy work decides how everything you built is protected and passed on. Trusts, Medicaid planning, and guardianships sit exactly at the junction of the two. One attorney across both means the plans actually match each other.
